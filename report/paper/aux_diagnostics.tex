answers four specific questions raised while reviewing the current draft: (1) why
do two cells show \texttt{n/a} across all four ILD quartiles in the paper's Table

\section{Quartile Binning: Is the Mechanism the Problem?}
\label{sec:quartile}

Table~\ref{tab:quartile-methods} compares three ways of computing the ILD$_{\text{norm}}$
quartile cut-points used for MMR's Q1--Q4 bins, on the two cells that currently show
\texttt{n/a} (LaMP-2/Base+Small/Contriever) plus two cells that work fine under the
current method, for contrast. All four quartiles are shown, not just Q1 --- Q1 is
the bin the paper's own claims hinge on, but a check of whether the \emph{mechanism}
is sound needs to see the whole distribution, not just the one slice we already
report.

\begin{description}
  \item[(A) Current --- cell-pooled:] quantile cut-points computed from that one
    (task, generator, ranker, method) cell's own pooled rows. This is what Table 2/6
    uses today.
  \item[(B) Dataset-wide:] \emph{one} global set of quantile cut-points, computed by
    pooling every MMR row across all 7 tasks $\times$ 4 cells together, then applied
    uniformly to every cell.
  \item[(C) Query-specific (rank-based):] within each query, rank that query's own
    $N$ MMR-$\lambda$ draws by ILD (percentile rank in $[0,1]$), then bin those
    \emph{ranks} globally at fixed $0/.25/.5/.75/1$ cuts. This never compares raw
    ILD values across different queries for bin assignment, so it cannot suffer the
    same cross-query tie-collision that breaks (A).
\end{description}

\textbf{Only (A) actually produces quartiles.} ``Quartile'' means equal-population
bins by definition, and only (A) delivers that, because its cut-points are fit to
each cell's own data (LaMP-4/Base/BM25: 1035/1034/1032/1034 --- as even as tie
handling allows). (B) applies cut-points fit to the \emph{global pooled} population
to each individual cell; if that cell's own distribution sits somewhere different
from the global one (common, since ILD scale varies a lot by task/generator/ranker),
the same fixed boundaries slice it very unevenly. (C) avoids that specific problem
but introduces its own: ties (Section~\ref{sec:quartile}'s root-cause discussion)
push many rows to the same middle rank, swelling Q2/Q3 and starving Q1/Q4. We
measured this directly across all 56 rows of each alternative (Section~\ref{sec:full-tables}):
the ratio between a row's largest and smallest bin averages $15\times$ under (B)
(worst case $200\times$, median $3.5\times$) and $2.8\times$ under (C) (worst case
$32\times$, median $1.1\times$ --- closer to balanced typically, but with a long
tail of bad cases). \textbf{Neither (B) nor (C) is a valid drop-in replacement for
``quartiles'' as a result} --- both are better read as alternative four-bin
schemes that happen to share the Q1--Q4 label, not as different ways of computing
the same underlying quantity. The Q1/Q4 columns below for (B) and (C) should be
read with that in mind, not as ``the least/most diversified quarter of this cell's
queries'' the way (A)'s columns can be.

\textbf{None of the three is a clean fix.} (A) fails outright on the two problem
cells --- across \emph{all four} bins, not just Q1, since it's one 3-way split for
the whole cell. (B)'s imbalance is visible directly in this table: the two problem
cells' Q1 drops to $n{=}3$--$5$ (too few to mean anything) while their Q4 sits at
$446$--$749$, and even an already-working cell (LaMP-7) sees its Q1 sample size
collapse from 265 (under A) to 81 while flipping from non-significant to nominally
significant ($p{<}0.05$) --- more a warning sign than an improvement. (C)'s
imbalance is visible too but milder for these 4 cells (LaMP-4: 1035 $\to$ 128 in
Q1, but Q2/Q3 swell to 1389/1850); it also surfaces one new result the Q1-only view
hid entirely: LaMP-2/Small/Contriever's Q2 is significantly \emph{negative} under
(C) ($n{=}184$, $-0.098^{*}$) --- a real finding this cell has, just not in the bin
the paper currently reports, though per the caveat above this ``Q2'' is not a clean
second-quarter-of-the-data the way Table 2/6's own Q2 is.

\paragraph{Root cause.} We checked directly how often a single query's own 5
MMR-$\lambda$ draws (0.3--0.85) produce the \emph{exact same} ILD$_{\text{norm}}$
value for all five: \textbf{46.4\% of LaMP-2/Base/Contriever's queries} and
\textbf{35.3\% of LaMP-4/Base/BM25's queries} (a cell that otherwise works fine)
have all five $\lambda$ settings tied. MMR's greedy selection is simply insensitive
to $\lambda$ for a large share of queries --- plausibly because the candidate pool
is small or homogeneous enough that the top-$k$ selection saturates regardless of
the relevance/diversity weighting. This is the same underlying property driving all
three failure modes above: (A) collapses when ties concentrate at the per-query
ceiling; (B) is fragile to any one cell's ILD scale differing from the global pool;
(C) loses samples to within-query ties landing at a tied, non-extreme rank. The
quartile mechanism is a symptom, not an independent bug --- switching binning
strategy relocates the problem rather than resolving it. LaMP-2/Contriever is simply
the most extreme instance of a tie rate that is high (30--45\%) across most
MMR cells to begin with.

\textbf{10\% does not continue that trend --- it reverses it, and not in a way
that inspires confidence.} Naively, tightening further should only make
significance \emph{harder} to reach (smaller $n$, same or more noise). Instead,
\textbf{5 of 16 cells are significant at 10\%, more than at 15\% (0) or Q1 (1)
combined} --- three new MMR positives on LaMP-4 (Base/BM25 $+0.049^{**}$,
Small/BM25 $+0.067^{**}$, Small/Contr.\ $+0.043^{*}$) and two new LaMP-1/Small
negatives (BM25/PL $-0.256^{*}$, Contr./MMR $-0.320^{**}$, the single largest
magnitude in the whole table). This is not evidence that 10\% is a
\emph{better-powered} cutoff --- it is evidence that the bottom decile is a
qualitatively different, smaller population, not just a stricter version of the
same one. We checked directly: at 10\%, LaMP-1/Small/Contr.'s MMR bin has 27
observations from only \textbf{8 unique queries} (of 51 in the cell) --- each
query contributing $\sim$3.4 samples on average, because a query whose ILD is
structurally low stays low across most of its $\lambda$ draws (the same tie
mechanism as Section~\ref{sec:quartile}'s root-cause discussion). At 15\% that
grows to 13 unique queries, at 25\% to 20; LaMP-4 cells show the same pattern
more mildly (e.g.\ LaMP-4/Base/BM25/MMR: 120 unique queries at 10\% vs.\ 279 at
25\%, out of 827 total). Welch's $t$-test assumes independent observations; a
handful of queries each contributing 3--4 correlated draws violates that, so the
$p$-values at 10\% are anti-conservative --- they look more significant than they
are. \textbf{The right reading is the opposite of the naive one}: the 10\%
results are \emph{less} trustworthy than 15\% or 25\%, not more, precisely
because they clear significance more often on a smaller, more repetitive query
pool. This reinforces \S\ref{sec:quartile}'s point rather than adding a new one
--- the least-diverse tail is dominated by a small set of structurally-low-ILD
queries, and how much of that tail you include changes both the point estimate
and how trustworthy the significance test on it actually is.

pooled across all four $\alpha$); its Table 2/6 bins by each cell's own

fairness/diversity/utility relationship, so we rebuilt both views from the

are five \emph{equal-width}, not equal-population, buckets, and they are far from
balanced: pooled across all 8 cells, \textbf{48.0\% of all 13{,}164 (qid,

\section{Bottom Line: Does More Diversity Buy More Utility?}
\label{sec:diversity-trend}

Every earlier section conditions on \emph{realized} per-query ILD to ask about
utility. This section asks the more direct question: for the setting itself (a
fixed PL $\alpha$ or MMR $\lambda$), does turning the diversity knob up predict a
better or worse $\Delta$EU$_{\text{norm}}$ against deterministic, pooled across all
7 tasks $\times$ 4 cells $= 28$ cells per method? We first confirmed empirically
which direction each knob actually moves realized diversity (mean
ILD$_{\text{norm}}$ of that setting's own output, averaged over the 28 cells):
$\alpha{=}1{\to}8$ gives $0.974 \to 0.966 \to 0.936 \to 0.928$; $\lambda{=}0.3{\to}0.85$
gives $0.986 \to 0.971 \to 0.960 \to 0.945 \to 0.938$. Both knobs behave as expected
and move diversity monotonically on average --- low $\alpha$/low $\lambda$ is the
more-diverse end, high $\alpha$/high $\lambda$ the less-diverse end closest to
deterministic.

\textbf{For PL: it is the low-$\alpha$/high-diversity half of the range that hurts,
not every $\alpha$.} $\alpha{=}1$ and $\alpha{=}2$ are both significantly negative

statistically indistinguishable from deterministic ($p{=}0.98$, $p{=}0.43$) ---
not worse, but not better either, just noise around zero. The within-cell slope
across the full range is still real: correlating each cell's own 4 $\alpha$ points'
realized ILD$_{\text{norm}}$ against their $\Delta$EU$_{\text{norm}}$ gives
\textbf{24 of 28 cells a negative slope} (sign test $p{=}0.00018$; pooling all
$28{\times}4{=}112$ points gives $\rho{=}-0.110$, $p{=}0.25$) --- but that slope is
driven almost entirely by $\alpha{=}1$/$2$ being bad, not by $\alpha{=}8$ being
good.

\textbf{For MMR, the pattern is even more concentrated in a single setting ---
and adding $\lambda{=}0.9$ and $\lambda{=}0.95$ does not change that.} Only
$\lambda{=}0.3$, the most-diverse MMR setting run, is significantly negative
($p{=}0.010$); $\lambda{=}0.45$ through $0.95$ are all statistically
indistinguishable from zero ($p{=}0.10$--$0.88$), including both new points
themselves ($p{=}0.72$ and $p{=}0.88$, essentially the same wash as
$\lambda{=}0.85$). Pooling all $28{\times}7{=}196$ points sits at
$\rho{=}-0.151$, $p{=}0.035$ --- nominally significant, similar to where it
landed with six $\lambda$'s ($\rho{=}-0.156$, $p{=}0.044$) but noticeably
different from five ($\rho{=}-0.164$, $p{=}0.053$). The per-cell sign test tells
a bumpier story across the same three additions: 18/8 of 26 non-flat cells
($p{=}0.076$) at five $\lambda$'s, 18/10 of 28 ($p{=}0.185$) at six, 19/8 of 27
non-flat ($p{=}0.052$) at seven --- non-monotonic, swinging back toward
significance rather than settling. That instability is itself the finding: the
per-cell sign test, the statistic we've argued throughout this section is the
right one to trust given the nested design, does not converge as more $\lambda$'s
are added, which means none of these individual $p$-values (marginal or pooled)
should be read as a stable estimate of anything --- the true per-cell relationship
is just noisy at $n{=}28$ cells, in both directions, regardless of how many
$\lambda$'s populate it. Individually, neither $\lambda{=}0.9$ nor $\lambda{=}0.95$
contributes a single new significant cell (still just the same two, both at
LaMP-4/Small/BM25, unchanged since five $\lambda$'s). MMR's diversity-utility
relationship is noisier and more task-dependent than PL's --- consistent with
Section~\ref{sec:mmr-vs-pl}'s finding that MMR's Q1 bin carries a real,
composition-independent effect that varies in \emph{both} directions by task,
unlike PL's.

\textbf{Bottom line:} in both methods, only the most-diverse end of the range we
tested is significantly worse than deterministic --- PL's $\alpha{\le}2$ and MMR's
$\lambda{=}0.3$ --- while every less-diverse setting (PL $\alpha{\ge}4$; MMR
$\lambda{\ge}0.45$, now confirmed out to $\lambda{=}0.95$) is statistically
indistinguishable from deterministic, not better. Checking every individual cell
too, not just the 28-cell marginal means: \textbf{zero} of PL's 112 (cell,
$\alpha$) combinations and \textbf{zero} of MMR's 196 (cell, $\lambda$)
combinations show a significantly \emph{positive} $\Delta$EU$_{\text{norm}}$ ---
every one of the 25 significant PL cells and both significant MMR cells is
negative. So: no, there is no $\alpha$ or $\lambda$, anywhere in the grid, that
reliably beats deterministic --- and pushing MMR all the way to $\lambda{=}0.95$
(realized ILD$_{\text{norm}}{=}0.931$, the closest any MMR setting gets to PL
$\alpha{=}8$'s $0.928$) does not surface one either; it is just another point in
the tied region. There is a large region that is statistically \emph{tied} with
deterministic (PL $\alpha{\ge}4$ everywhere; MMR at any $\lambda{\ge}0.45$, and
even $\lambda{=}0.3$ in most individual cells), plus a smaller region (PL's
most-diverse settings, and MMR pushed hard at $\lambda{=}0.3$) that is reliably
worse. The honest summary is ``enough diversity is measurably costly; anything
short of that is free, not beneficial,'' not ``some amount of diversity helps.''
