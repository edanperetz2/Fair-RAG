\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Fairness-aware stochastic rerankers are usually expected to trade ranking quality for
more equitable item exposure, yet Eun Kim and Diaz (2025) report that RAG systems
built on such rerankers can maintain, and sometimes exceed, the generation utility of
deterministic ranking. We reproduce this setup on the LaMP benchmark and investigate
the mechanism behind it, using Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) -- a deterministic
diversity-relevance re-ranker -- alongside Plackett-Luce (PL) sampling so that
diversification can be studied separately from the randomness of stochastic ranking
itself. We find a real but narrow and asymmetric effect: in the least-diversified
quintile of each method's own output, MMR's utility significantly exceeds the
deterministic baseline in 4 of 7 LaMP tasks, while PL's blind randomization does so in
only 1 of 7 -- evidence that where diversification helps, it is mostly MMR's
relevance-aware selection doing the work, not randomization itself. We nonetheless
propose a hybrid reranking method, RandMMR -- PL sampling of the top-ranked item
followed by a randomized MMR pass over the remaining ranks -- that drives Expected
Exposure Disparity far below both the deterministic baseline and any plain-PL setting
reaching a comparable disparity level, while matching or exceeding plain PL's utility
in 17 of 24 tested (task, generator, retriever) cells. We also offer a critical
reading of both the original paper's evaluation protocol -- most notably its reliance
on a stochastic oracle for utility normalization that is expensive to reproduce
faithfully -- and of the LaMP benchmark itself as ground truth for this kind of study:
its relevance labels come from a single generator's utility delta rather than fixed
human judgment, its candidate pools are small with an unusually high useful-document
ratio next to classical retrieval collections, our generators are far smaller than
typical production RAG systems, and we find direct evidence that PL's
fairness/ranking-quality trade-off is not a per-query guarantee -- individual queries'
relevance-weighted exposure exceeds the deterministic ranking's roughly a third of the
time even though the trade-off holds on average. We lay out concrete directions for
future work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:intro}

Fair-ranking research typically formalizes an unavoidable trade-off: enforcing more
equitable exposure among relevant items costs some ranking quality relative to a
purely relevance-ordered list \cite{castillo2018fairness,diaz2020evaluating}.
Eun Kim and Diaz \cite{eunkim2025fairrag} extend this literature to
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), reporting that RAG systems using a stochastic,
fairness-aware retriever policy can maintain, and in some regimes even exceed, the
generation utility of a fully deterministic retriever, despite that trade-off. This
motivates three questions, the first two stated in our original project proposal:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1em]
  \item \textbf{RQ1 (Reproduction).} Does the original paper's fairness-utility
  relationship hold under an independent re-implementation, and are there
  unmentioned limitations or sensitivities in how it is measured?
  \item \textbf{RQ2 (Mechanism).} If fairness interventions do not cost utility, is
  this because they increase the \emph{diversity} of retrieved items --- intuitively,
  a generator given a less redundant document set has more distinct evidence to draw
  on?
  \item \textbf{RQ3 (Improvement).} Given whatever RQ1--RQ2 reveal about \emph{why}
  fairness interventions do not cost utility, can that insight be used to design a
  reranking policy that pushes fairness (lower EE-D) further than the settings we
  study reach on their own, without giving utility back up in exchange?
\end{itemize}

Formally, following \cite{eunkim2025fairrag}, a RAG system is a tuple
$\langle R, G, k \rangle$ of a retriever $R$, generator $G$, and truncation depth
$k$. A deterministic $R$ returns one ranked list per query; a stochastic policy $S$
instead samples a ranking $\pi \sim S(\cdot \mid q)$, and system quality is reported
in expectation over $N$ sampled rankings: Expected Exposure Disparity/Relevance
(EE-D/EE-R, item-side ranking fairness and quality) and Expected Utility (EU,
downstream generation quality), each normalized to a theoretical or empirical bound
per query \cite{diaz2020evaluating,eunkim2025fairrag}. We add one further quantity
not defined in the source paper: \emph{intra-list diversity}
$\mathrm{ILD}(\pi) = 1 - \overline{\mathrm{Jaccard}}(\pi)$, the mean pairwise
token-set dissimilarity of the $k$ retrieved items, following the standard
similarity-vs-diversity framing of \cite{smyth2001similarity}. RQ2 asks whether EU is
better predicted by ILD than by the fairness dial itself.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Fair and stochastic ranking.} Fairness in ranking is not simply fairness in
classification applied item-by-item: a ranked list's effect on an item is mediated by
\emph{exposure}, computed via the position-weighted formula defined by Diaz et al.\
\cite{diaz2020evaluating} rather than by an item's bare relevance. Item-side exposure
fairness is formalized via the Expected Exposure framework \cite{diaz2020evaluating}, which
measures a policy's exposure allocation against a fair target using a stochastic
ranking sampled by the Plackett-Luce (PL) model
\cite{plackett1975analysis,luce1959individual}; the temperature parameter $\alpha$
that we sweep here controls how close PL sampling is to the deterministic base
ranking. Castillo \cite{castillo2018fairness} surveys the fairness-transparency
trade-off in rankings that motivates this line of work. \emph{Towards Fair RAG}
\cite{eunkim2025fairrag} is the paper under reproduction: it applies this fairness
framework to RAG, measuring EE-D/EE-R/EU across LaMP tasks and several
retriever-generator pairs, and is the direct source of our experimental design,
metrics, and normalization convention (Appendix C of \cite{eunkim2025fairrag}). Its
utility metric, EU, is computed per sampled ranking as the downstream generator's
task-appropriate score (accuracy, MAE, or ROUGE-L, depending on the LaMP task) when
conditioned on that ranking's retrieved items, then normalized per query against an
oracle-ranking upper bound before being averaged across $N$ sampled rankings.

\textbf{Diversity in retrieval.} Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)
\cite{carbonell1998use} is the classic deterministic diversity-relevance trade-off
re-ranker, greedily selecting each next item to maximize $\lambda \cdot
\mathrm{relevance} - (1-\lambda)\cdot\max\text{-similarity-to-selected}$; we use it
as a second, non-stochastic diversification arm alongside PL. Intra-list diversity
via pairwise dissimilarity follows \cite{smyth2001similarity}.

\textbf{Personalization benchmark and models.} We use LaMP
\cite{salemi2023lamp} as our dataset (Section~\ref{sec:data}) and Flan-T5
\cite{chung2024scaling} as our generator family, matching \cite{eunkim2025fairrag}.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:data}

We use LaMP \cite{salemi2023lamp}, the same benchmark as \cite{eunkim2025fairrag},
for two reasons. First, and primarily, paper-faithfulness: LaMP is the only dataset
for which the original paper published per-task, per-generator utility-labeled
corpora (filtered to items with a measurable generator-specific utility delta), so
reproducing on it is the only way to compare our numbers directly against theirs
rather than against a different task distribution. Second, tractability: LaMP's
candidate pools are small enough that deriving actual relevance labels is
computationally feasible in the first place (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), which gives
us, for the same queries, both a retrieval-fairness signal (which documents are
labeled useful) and a generation-correctness signal --- a joint
retrieval-\emph{and}-generation labeling that,
to our knowledge, is not available together in other RAG-evaluation datasets, and is
exactly what studying fairness in RAG needs. LaMP additionally spans seven
personalization tasks with heterogeneous metrics (accuracy, MAE, ROUGE-L), letting us
check whether any finding is task-specific or general.

We verified our local corpus against the original paper's own published statistics
(Appendix D, Tables 4--5): per-task query count, average candidate-document count,
average useful-document count, and average useful-document percentage all match
to the paper's reported precision across all 7 tasks $\times$ 2 generators (28
cells), confirming our filtered dataset is the identical one used upstream, not a
re-derived approximation.

We run two generators (Flan-T5-Small, Flan-T5-Base \cite{chung2024scaling}) and two
retrievers (BM25, Contriever), against the paper's larger grid (adding SPLADE and
two larger generators) --- a scope reduction driven by available compute, not
methodology. Reranking settings are deterministic, a gold/oracle ranker, PL at
$\alpha \in \{1,2,4,8\}$, and MMR at $\lambda \in \{0.15,0.3,0.45,0.55,0.7,0.85\}$,
all truncated to $k=5$, matching \cite{eunkim2025fairrag}'s reported $k$.

These reasons for choosing LaMP come with limitations worth stating up front, shared
with the source paper and not introduced by our reproduction, since they bound how
far any effect we measure in the results that follow (Section~\ref{sec:results}),
including RQ1's reproduction and RQ2's diversity test, should generalize.

\textbf{Relevance is a per-generator utility delta, not a fixed judgment.} Unlike
TREC-style collections, where a document's relevance is a human assessor's judgment
fixed once and shared by every system evaluated against it, a LaMP candidate document
is labeled relevant iff conditioning generation on it, versus a zero-context
baseline, \emph{improves that specific generator's} task metric
($\delta = \text{augment\_score} - \text{baseline\_score}$; label $=1$ if
$\delta{>}0$, else $0$). Ground truth is therefore generator-specific, single-sample (no
repeated-trial averaging over the scoring judgment itself), and not portable across
models: the same document can be ``relevant'' for one generator and ``irrelevant''
for another on the identical query. Table~\ref{tab:corpus}'s two rows are not
directly comparable this way, though, since each generator's own utility-delta
filtering keeps a different, mostly non-overlapping query subset --- LaMP-1 keeps 51
queries for Flan-T5-Small and 232 for Flan-T5-Base, only 21 in both. Restricting to
those 21 shared queries (identical candidate pools on both sides), 22.8\% of paired
document judgments flip between the two generators' labels, and the useful-document
rate itself still differs sharply on this identical subset (9.4\% under
Flan-T5-Small vs.\ 20.2\% under Flan-T5-Base). Any fairness-ranking result computed
against these labels inherits this:
it is a statement about ranking relative to \emph{one model's} learned notion of
usefulness, not a model-agnostic ground truth.

\textbf{The corpus is small, with an unusually high useful-document rate.} Table
\ref{tab:corpus} gives per-task, per-generator candidate-pool and useful-document
statistics. Candidate pools range 19--192 documents/query (mean $\sim$110); the
useful-document rate ranges 9.5\%--45.5\% (mean $\sim$29\%). Classical TREC ad hoc
collections typically retrieve from candidate pools several orders of magnitude
larger, with relevance rates well under 1\%. A ranking-fairness metric's behavior at
$k{=}5$ --- the truncation depth reported by \cite{eunkim2025fairrag} and, in turn,
the only depth used anywhere in our own experiments --- depends heavily on how much
of the candidate pool is actually useful: with a 30\% useful rate and $k{=}5$, a
policy that samples uniformly at random from the top of the pool already has decent
odds of surfacing several useful documents, which compresses the
deterministic-vs-stochastic utility gap the whole fairness/utility trade-off is
measured against, relative to a $<$1\%-relevance TREC-scale setting.

\textbf{Generators on both sides are small and dated by industry standards.}
Flan-T5-Small (77M) and Flan-T5-Base (250M) \cite{chung2024scaling}, the two we run,
are two to three orders of magnitude smaller than generators typically used in
production RAG systems ($\geq$3B parameters, e.g.\ Llama-3-8B- or GPT-4o-mini-class
models). This is not only a limitation of our reduced grid: the source paper's own
larger generators, up to Flan-T5-XXL (11B), are from the same 2022-era Flan-T5
family \cite{chung2024scaling} --- still encoder-decoder, not instruction- or
task-finetuned beyond LaMP's own prompt format, and now several generations behind
the decoder-only models that dominate deployed RAG. Whether the fairness/utility
relationships either paper measures --- e.g.\ RQ2's diversity effect here --- persist
at production model scale and architecture is untested by both (see Future Work).

\textbf{The EE-D/EE-R trade-off is not a per-query guarantee.} The same small,
high-relevance-rate corpus (previous point) lets PL's aggregate fairness/quality
trade-off fail query-by-query even while holding on average: across all completed PL
conditions ($\alpha \in \{1,2,4,8\}$, both generators, both retrievers, LaMP-1--7),
an individual query's PL EE-R exceeds its own deterministic EE-R in 29.7\% of
query-level observations --- 37--38\% at $\alpha \in \{1,2,4\}$, still 8.3\% at the
mildest, near-deterministic $\alpha{=}8$ --- though this mostly washes out once
pooled to a macro (task, generator, ranker, $\alpha$) mean (14 of 112 conditions,
all at $\alpha{=}8$ and $\Delta \leq 0.004$). A caution against over-interpreting any
single query's or single run's fairness/quality numbers.

\section{Methodology Notes}
\label{sec:method}

We treat the original paper's open-sourced code as a direct reference throughout:
matching its library versions, reusing its utility-labeling technique
(Section~\ref{sec:threats}) and implementation details (prompt templates, scoring
functions) wherever our framework's structure allowed it directly, and otherwise
re-implementing to match its stated method as closely as we could. We report
divergences from the paper's own numbers (Section~\ref{sec:results}) as limitations
of exact reproduction under a reduced compute grid, not as bugs in either codebase,
since an extensive audit (prompt/scoring-function parity, generation parity against
the paper's own pipeline, model-version and seed invariance checks) found no
implementation-level explanation for them.

For the diversity-mediation test (RQ2), for each LaMP task we bin PL's and MMR's own
generated lists \emph{separately} into ILD$_{\text{norm}}$ quintiles and compare the
least-diversified quintile's (Q1) mean EU$_{\text{norm}}$ against the deterministic
baseline's, via a two-sided Welch's $t$-test, task-stratified with equal weight across
all four generator$\times$retriever cells. One MMR setting, $\lambda{=}1.0$, is
excluded: at $\lambda{=}1$ MMR's diversity term has zero weight, so its greedy
selection provably (and empirically, 100/100 on a real sample) reproduces the
deterministic ranking exactly --- it is not a diversification condition at all. A
second precision control matters at this scale: some (generator, ranker, $\alpha$)
conditions have been resampled more times ($N{\in}\{20,30\}$) than others ($N{=}10$),
and pooling each condition at its own maximum $N$ independently would silently give
some conditions tighter per-query utility estimates than the ones they are compared
against. Every PL-derived number in Section~\ref{sec:results} instead pools all
conditions inside one task at a single $N$ shared by every (generator, ranker,
$\alpha$) triple for that task --- the highest such $N$, or $N{=}10$ where no higher
value is universal.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{RQ1: Reproduction fidelity}

Our reproduction of the paper's Figure 4 (EE-D$\leftrightarrow$EE-R$\leftrightarrow$EU
relationships) and Table 2 (disparity-interval utility deltas) recovers the same
qualitative trends the paper reports, once curve construction is matched exactly to
its stated method (binning every individual sampled list into disparity intervals
rather than averaging per $\alpha$-condition first, confirmed by pixel-level
inspection of the published figure): a fairness/ranking-quality trade-off that rises
then plateaus, a clean positive ranking-quality/utility relationship, and a similar
disparity-interval utility pattern to the paper's own Table 2. Absolute utility scale
is not directly comparable, since the paper's ceiling is a genuine stochastic oracle
(multiple sampled optimal permutations) while ours is a single deterministic oracle
draw (Future Work).

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} compares our reproduced deterministic-baseline EU against the
two tasks for which the paper publishes baseline values directly. Flan-T5-Base is
within 4\% on both. Flan-T5-Small reproduces 22--30\% \emph{below} the paper on
LaMP-1 --- traced to a genuine, content-invariant model failure mode (it predicts the
same class label regardless of retrieved context on this task) --- and 38--42\%
\emph{above} on LaMP-4, consistent with our single-draw normalization ceiling being
systematically looser than the paper's true stochastic oracle. We treat both as
reproduction-fidelity limitations under a reduced compute grid, not as errors in
either codebase.

\label{sec:rq2}

The key test is whether this diversity increase predicts \emph{higher} utility.
Figure~\ref{fig:ild-sweetspot} bins PL's and MMR's own lists separately into

deterministic mean as reference. Both methods peak at Q1 --- not an extreme corner of
the absolute diversity range, since even ``least diversified'' PL/MMR output is
usually still measurably more diverse than a bare top-$k$ list, given how small LaMP's
candidate pools are (Section~\ref{sec:threats}) --- but the effect is asymmetric:
MMR's Q1 mean (0.528) clears the deterministic reference (0.507) with room to spare,
while PL's Q1 mean (0.507) is essentially flush with it.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_mmr_vs_pl_q1.pdf}
\caption{$\Delta$EU$_{\text{norm}}$ vs.\ deterministic at the Q1 (least-diversified)

Figure~\ref{fig:mmr-vs-pl-q1} breaks this down per task: MMR's Q1 delta vs.\
deterministic is positive in 5 of 7 tasks and significant in 4 (LaMP-1, LaMP-3,
LaMP-4, LaMP-6; all $p{<}0.01$), with the remaining two negligible rather than
meaningfully negative (LaMP-5: $-0.004$; LaMP-7: $\approx 0$). PL's Q1 delta is
positive in only 3 of 7 tasks and significant in just one (LaMP-3, $p{<}0.001$).
Where diversification helps at all, in other words, it is mostly MMR doing the work:
its relevance-aware selection retains more of what already made the original ranking
good, while PL's blind randomization mostly just adds noise.

This is a real but narrow finding, not a broad confirmation of the
diversity-mediation hypothesis in our original proposal: the benefit is concentrated
in a specific diversity range (mild, not maximal), is far stronger for MMR than PL,
and even for MMR is significant in barely more than half the tasks we test. It does
support a more specific claim: mild, relevance-aware diversification (MMR) is a real,
if task-dependent, source of utility gain over deterministic ranking, while PL's
fairness-motivated randomization, on its own, mostly is not.

\subsection{RQ3: RandMMR, a hybrid PL + randomized-MMR reranker}
\label{sec:rq3}

Motivated by RQ1--RQ2 --- fairness interventions do not reliably cost utility, and
mild MMR-style diversification is a real (if partial) reason why --- we propose
\textbf{RandMMR}: PL sampling of the top-ranked item, then a randomized-MMR pass
($\lambda \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\ell_{lo},\ell_{hi})$) over the remaining ranks. We
evaluate the RandMMR condition run across the most tasks and cells ($\alpha{=}4$,
$\lambda\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0.8,1.0)$, $N{=}20$: LaMP-$\{1,2,3,4,5,7\}$ $\times$ 4
generator/retriever cells, 24 completed conditions) against plain PL, choosing per
cell the PL $\alpha \in \{1,2,4,8\}$ whose own mean EE-D is closest to RandMMR's ---
biased toward an $\alpha$ with the same or slightly \emph{higher} disparity than
RandMMR's own, so PL never gets an unearned fairness advantage in the comparison ---
unless the single closest $\alpha$ is already within 0.02 EE-D, in which case that one
is used regardless of direction.

direction is consistent (17 of 24, no significant losses) and matches the LaMP-1
multi-$\alpha$ picture in Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid}: RandMMR is, at minimum, not costing
utility relative to comparably-fair plain PL, and appears to more often help than not.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

\textbf{RQ1.} The qualitative shape of the paper's central relationships (fairness
vs.\ ranking quality; ranking quality vs.\ utility) reproduces once curve
construction is matched exactly to the paper's method. Absolute utility numbers,
however, diverge meaningfully despite an extensive fidelity audit finding no
implementation, data, model-version, or seed-related explanation --- a
$\sim$25\% shortfall specific to LaMP-1/Flan-T5-Small, traced to a genuine,
content-invariant model failure mode, and a uniform $\sim$35--42\% excess across
\emph{all four} LaMP-4 cells, most plausibly a looser empirical-max normalization
ceiling on our side (a single deterministic oracle draw vs.\ the paper's true
stochastic multi-sample oracle) rather than a per-model artifact. We report both as
limitations of exact reproduction under a reduced retriever/generator grid rather
than as errors in either codebase.

\textbf{RQ2.} We find a narrow, asymmetric form of support for the
diversity-mediation hypothesis in our proposal, not the broad, uniform version it was
originally framed as. Across 177{,}718 individual query-level PL/MMR observations
(Section~\ref{sec:rq2}), MMR's least-diversified quintile significantly exceeds
deterministic utility in 4 of 7 tasks; PL's does so in only 1. Diversification helps
where it helps mostly through MMR's relevance-aware selection, mildly applied, not
through PL's fairness-motivated randomization on its own, and even then only in about
half the tasks we test.

\textbf{RQ3.} We proposed RandMMR, a hybrid policy motivated by RQ1--RQ2: PL sampling
of the top-ranked item, then a randomized-MMR pass over the remaining ranks.
Evaluated against plain PL at a matched (or conservatively, slightly worse) fairness
level across 24 completed cells spanning six LaMP tasks (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}),
RandMMR's normalized EU exceeds the matched PL's in 17 of 24 cells (71\%),
significantly in one, with no cell showing a significant PL advantage; a richer
LaMP-1 view across RandMMR's own $\alpha$ sweep shows it reaching EE-D well below any
workable plain-PL setting except $\alpha{=}1$, which it matches or beats in utility
throughout. We read this as encouraging rather than conclusive --- the direction is
consistent and the fairness gain is large, but the grid is not yet complete and only
one cell reaches significance individually.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.1em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item Explore other hybrid reranking policies beyond RandMMR's specific
  PL-rank-1-then-randomized-MMR-tail design (e.g., varying which ranks get which
  treatment, or blending in an explicit relevance floor).
  \item Explore other diversity/fairness reranking methods entirely, e.g.\
  xQuAD-style explicit sub-topic coverage, as a further point of comparison to MMR
  and PL.
  \item Run with larger sample counts, especially for the under-powered
  Flan-T5-Small RandMMR cells and the tasks outside our 24-cell grid
  (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
  \item Acquire or construct a more stable dataset/ground-truth for fairness-in-RAG
  evaluation, less dependent on a single generator's utility delta for relevance
  (Section~\ref{sec:threats}).
  \item Repeat with stronger, newer generators actually used in production RAG
  systems ($\geq$3B parameters, often task-finetuned) and with SPLADE as a third
  retriever, to test whether our findings are small-model/small-grid artifacts.
  \item A properly stochastic oracle retriever (multiple sampled optimal
  permutations, as specified in the paper's Appendix C.3, vs.\ our single
  deterministic draw) would tighten the utility-normalization ceiling behind
  Table~\ref{tab:baseline}.
  \item A causal mediation analysis (e.g., instrumenting diversity directly rather
  than reading it off realized lists) would be a stronger test of RQ2 than the
  quintile-comparison design used here, which remains correlational.
  \item The paper's Figure 5 (NLI-based attribution fairness) requires an entailment
  pipeline we did not build and is a natural extension once RQ1's open discrepancies
  are resolved.
  \item Tackle optimization of diversity/fairness toggles (PL $\alpha$, MMR
  $\lambda$) customized per dataset, task, or specific information need, rather than
  one fixed setting swept globally --- directly motivated by RQ2's finding that the
  effect is task-specific.
  \item Sweep other parameters we held fixed throughout at the source paper's own
  reported value, most notably the truncation depth $k{=}5$ (Section~\ref{sec:data}):
  since several of this paper's own findings (the corpus-size caveat above; RQ2's
  quintile effect) are plausibly $k$-dependent, checking whether they hold at other
  depths is a natural robustness check we did not have scope for here.
\end{enumerate}
